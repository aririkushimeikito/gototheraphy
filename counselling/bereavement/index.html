About bereavement and loss

What bereavement tends to feel like

Grief does not follow a timetable, whatever people around you may expect. It can arrive months after a death, or fade and return without warning. It can feel like sadness, but also like anger, numbness, guilt, relief, or a strange practical calm. It can be for a person, but also for a relationship, a home, a job, a pregnancy or a version of your own future.

Many people find that the world moves on faster than they can. The cards stop. The phone goes quiet. That is often the point at which talking to somebody outside your life starts to make sense.

When it is worth talking to somebody

There is no point at which grief becomes serious enough to deserve help. If you want somewhere to say the things you cannot say to the people who are also grieving, a therapist can be that place. You do not need to be struggling to cope. You are allowed to want an hour where nobody needs you to be all right.

What kinds of therapy people work with after a loss

Many counsellors work with bereavement as a core part of their practice, often using person-centred or humanistic approaches that give you room to talk without being directed. Some describe their work as psychodynamic, which pays attention to what the loss has stirred up from earlier in your life. Some work in a shorter, more structured way. Profiles here say which, in the therapist's own words, and nothing on this page ranks one above another.

What a first session tends to cover

A first session after a loss is usually slow. The therapist will want to know who or what you have lost, and what has happened since. You can say as little as you want. You can also ask how they work with grief and whether they have a sense of how long people tend to see them. You do not have to decide anything, and you do not have to come back.
