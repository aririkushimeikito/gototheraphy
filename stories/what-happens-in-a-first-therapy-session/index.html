Starting

What happens in a first therapy session?

By Gototherapy editorial6 min readNot a testimonial. Not medical advice.Clinical reviewer required before publish

A first therapy session is a conversation about what has brought you, how long it has been going on, and what you would like to be different. The therapist asks questions, explains how they work, and answers yours. Nothing is decided in the room, and you are free not to go back.

What is the first session actually for?

It is for two people finding out whether they can work together. That is the whole job of the first hour. It is not an assessment you can fail, and it is not the start of a fixed course. Some therapists call it an initial consultation, some an assessment session, some nothing at all. Whatever it is called, the shape is similar.

You will probably have been asked to arrive a few minutes early, or to join a video call at a set time. There may be a short form about contact details and who to call in an emergency. There should not be a form asking you to explain your problem in writing before you have met.

What will the therapist ask?

Usually something open. What has brought you here. What has been happening. Some therapists ask how long it has been going on and whether anything in particular made you get in touch now rather than six months ago. Some ask what you have tried already, including whether you have had therapy before and what that was like.

You may be asked about your life more broadly: work, home, who you live with, how you sleep. This is not small talk. It is the therapist trying to understand the room you live in, not only the thing you came about.

You do not have to answer anything. “I would rather not go into that yet” is an ordinary sentence in a first session and no therapist worth seeing will push past it.

What should I ask them?

Whatever you want to know. Three questions are useful for almost everyone. How do you work, in plain English. How often would you expect to see me, and for roughly how long. What happens if I want to stop.

If cost has not already been agreed in writing, ask. You are allowed to ask about money before anything else, and a therapist will not think less of you for it. Ask what happens if you need to cancel, and how much notice they need.

If the therapist uses a word you do not understand, ask what it means. A therapist who explains “psychodynamic” or “person-centred” in a sentence is telling you something about how the rest of the sessions will go.

What if I do not know where to start?

Say that. It is the most common first sentence there is. Many people spend the first ten minutes talking about the traffic, or apologising for not being able to explain themselves, and the therapist is used to it. You do not have to be articulate about the thing that is wrong. That is partly what the sessions are for.

Some people write a few lines on their phone beforehand and read them out. Some bring nothing and see what comes. Neither is better.

Will I have to talk about my childhood?

Only if you want to, and only if it is relevant to how the therapist works. Some approaches pay close attention to early life. Others stay with what is happening now. A profile on Gototherapy explains the therapist's approach in plain English, so you can see before you contact them whether that is the kind of conversation you want.

What happens at the end of the first session?

The therapist will usually say something about whether they think they can help, and what they would suggest. You do not have to decide anything on the spot. Most therapists expect you to go away and think about it, and will say so. If they press you to commit to a block of sessions before you have left the room, that is worth noticing.

It is normal to feel tired afterwards, or oddly lighter, or nothing much. It is also normal to replay it and wish you had said something differently. None of that tells you whether it was the right person.

How do I know if they are the right person?

You may not know after one session. After two or three, ask yourself whether you feel listened to, whether you could tell them when something they said did not land, and whether they explain what they are doing when you ask. If any of those is a no, say so in the session. If it does not change, it is reasonable to try somebody else. Changing therapist is not a failure and needs no justification.

What if I cannot afford private therapy?

You should not leave with nothing. In England, you can refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies for anxiety and depression without going through a GP, by finding your local service on the NHS website. A GP can also refer you, and can tell you about other local services. In Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, your GP is the usual route. Waiting times vary by area and the service will tell you what to expect.

What if it is not working?

Say so to the therapist. It is one of the most useful things you can say, and a good therapist will want to hear it. If nothing changes after you have raised it, you are allowed to stop, and you are allowed to look for somebody else without explaining why. How to tell whether therapy is working goes into this in more detail.

When you are ready to look, every therapist listed here has been checked by a person, and their profile answers the questions above before you ever get in touch. Read two or three, and contact the one you think you could talk to.
