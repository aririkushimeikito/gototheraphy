Keeping going

How do I know if therapy is working?

By Gototherapy editorial6 min readNot a testimonial. Not medical advice.Clinical reviewer required before publish

Therapy is usually working if, over a few weeks, you find you can say more in the room than you could at the start, you feel listened to rather than managed, and you notice small differences outside the sessions. It rarely feels like progress from the inside. If none of that is happening, say so to the therapist.

Why does progress in therapy feel invisible?

Because it is slow, uneven and mostly happens between sessions. The changes people report later are often ordinary: they replied to an email they had been avoiding, they slept through a night, they did not take the argument home with them. None of those feels like a breakthrough at the time. Some weeks feel worse than the week before, because you are talking about something you had been keeping shut.

Measuring therapy against how you feel walking out of the door is unreliable. A difficult session is not a bad one, and a pleasant session is not necessarily a useful one.

What are the signs that it is working?

Three are worth watching for after the first month or so.

You can say more. Things you would not have mentioned in the first session come up without your deciding to raise them. You correct the therapist when they get something wrong, and it does not feel like a risk.

You feel listened to. Not agreed with, not reassured, but understood. The therapist remembers what you told them. When they get it wrong, they notice, or they take it well when you point it out.

Something is different outside. Not necessarily the thing you came about. People often notice they are a little quicker to catch themselves, a little slower to react, a little more able to say what they want. Ask somebody who knows you whether they have noticed anything; they often see it before you do.

What does it look like when it is not working?

You leave every session feeling worse and it never lifts. You find yourself performing for the therapist, giving the answers you think they want. You have stopped saying the things that matter because of how they responded last time. The therapist talks more than you do, or keeps steering back to their own framework when you want to go somewhere else. You dread going, not in the ordinary way of dreading something hard, but in the way of dreading something pointless.

One of these, once, means nothing. Several of them for several weeks are worth taking seriously.

Should I tell my therapist that it is not working?

Yes. It is one of the most useful things you can say in a session, and a good therapist will want to hear it. Many people find that the conversation about what is not working turns into the most useful session they have had, because it is finally about the two of you in the room rather than about the past.

You do not have to be diplomatic. “I am not sure this is helping” is enough. So is “I feel like I am performing.” If the therapist responds by defending themselves, or by explaining that this is a normal stage you have to push through without asking you anything, that is information.

How long should I give it?

There is no number that applies to everyone, and you should be suspicious of anyone who gives you one. Some therapists work in short, structured contracts of a set number of sessions; if that is what you agreed, review at the point you agreed to review. Open-ended work is harder to judge. A reasonable approach is to raise your doubts as soon as you have them, and if nothing has changed a few sessions after you raised them, to decide.

Is it the therapist, or is it me?

Often it is neither. Two competent people can simply not fit. The approach may be wrong for you: some people need structure and tasks, others need room and silence, and a therapist who is excellent at one may not offer the other. The timing may be wrong. Something in your life may have changed since you started.

Changing therapist is not a failure, and you do not owe anyone a justification. It is also not a reason to stop looking. People who found the second or third therapist right often say the first one taught them what they needed.

When should I stop?

When you have what you came for, or when you have decided this is not the place to get it. Both are legitimate endings. Ideally, give a session's notice rather than disappearing, not for the therapist's sake but for yours: an ending you chose and said out loud tends to sit better than one you slid out of.

You are allowed to not come back. That was true after the first session and it is true now.

What if I cannot pay for more?

Say so. Some therapists keep a small number of reduced-fee places and will tell you if one is available. If private therapy has stopped being possible, in England you can refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies without a GP, and your GP can tell you about other local services anywhere in the UK. Stopping because of money is not a verdict on you or on the therapy.
